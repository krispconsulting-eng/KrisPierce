## LinkedIn post 1: the gap

Six in ten caregivers have low wellbeing.

The 2025 Carer Wellbeing Survey, run by the University of Canberra for Carers Australia, had 10,918 responses. It found that 61.1% of unpaid caregivers had low wellbeing. For Australian adults overall, the figure was 33.6%. A year earlier the figure for caregivers was 57.7%.

So the gap is large, and it grew.

I have been a caregiver for 20 years. What I notice is that most support asks caregivers to do more: book, attend, complete. People who are stretched often cannot.

I think the useful question for organisations is a simple one. What can we offer that asks almost nothing of the person receiving it?

I am working on one answer, called Caregiver Wellbeing. More on that soon.

Source: 2025 Carer Wellbeing Survey, University of Canberra for Carers Australia.

## LinkedIn post 2: caregivers at work

Some of your staff are caregivers. You may not know which ones.

About 3.0 million Australians care for someone without pay. That is almost 12% of people living in households (Australian Bureau of Statistics, Survey of Disability, Ageing and Carers 2022).

Caring and paid work pull against each other. In 2018 the employment-to-population ratio was 55.8% for primary caregivers and 77.4% for people who were not caregivers (Australian Bureau of Statistics, Survey of Disability, Ageing and Carers 2018).

Leave policies and flexible hours matter. So does something for the person, not only the role.

Three questions worth asking in your organisation:

1. Do staff know it is safe to say they are a caregiver?
2. What do we offer that is about their own wellbeing?
3. Can they use it without their manager knowing?

## LinkedIn post 3: what a funder should and should not receive

If an organisation funds wellbeing support for caregivers, what should it get back?

My answer: a report about the group, and nothing about any person.

That means:

- Aggregate results only.
- No figure for any group smaller than ten people.
- No names, and no way of knowing who took part.
- No say over the content.
- No product presence.
- Support disclosed in plain words.

This protects caregivers. It also protects the funder, because a programme that people trust is a programme people use.

It asks something of funders too: accepting an honest report, including how many people did not finish, and what a small pilot cannot show.

I would be glad to hear how other organisations handle this.

## LinkedIn post 4: 2027

2027 is a year of decisions for caregiver support in Australia.

- Carer Gateway provider agreements end on 30 June 2027, with a new grant opportunity from 1 July 2027 (Department of Health, Disability and Ageing grant opportunity materials).
- The first action plan under the National Carer Strategy runs to December 2027 (National Carer Strategy Action Plan 2024 to 2027).
- Victoria's Support for Carers Program has a funding round for 2027 to 2031 (Victorian Government).

Organisations writing plans and bids this year are deciding what caregivers will be offered for years to come.

Digital programmes for caregivers have shown short-term reductions in stress and burden in published meta-analyses (Sherifali and colleagues, JMIR, 2018), though many of those studies carry a high risk of bias. Informed by evidence, and honest about its limits, is the right standard.

If you are working on this, I would like to compare notes.
