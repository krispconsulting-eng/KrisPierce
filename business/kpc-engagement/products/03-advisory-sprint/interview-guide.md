# Strategic Advisory Sprint: interview guide

Internal. Up to six interviews, 45 minutes each, online, with 15 minutes of notes straight afterwards. One core guide used every time, plus a set of questions for each of the four sprint types and a short set for interviews with consumers and community members.

## 1. Before each interview

- [ ] The interviewee has had the invitation (`emails.md`, 2) and knows the decision the sprint serves.
- [ ] Kris knows why this person is on the list (the reason recorded at kickoff).
- [ ] Three questions from the evidence log that only this person can answer are marked.
- [ ] For a consumer, carer or community member: payment is arranged at the agreed published rate, they know the amount and when it arrives, and they have been offered the questions in advance and a support person if they want one.

## 2. Core guide (every interview)

| Minutes | Part | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 5 | Opening and consent | Trust and ground rules |
| 5 to 12 | Their view of the situation | How they see it, in their words |
| 12 to 30 | Questions for the sprint type | Evidence for the findings |
| 30 to 40 | Options and trade-offs | Test the candidate options |
| 40 to 45 | Close | What was missed; what happens next |

**Opening (say all of it).**
"Thank you for making the time. [Organisation] has asked me to help with one decision: [decision]. I am speaking with up to six people. What you tell me is reported without your name, and I will not attribute a view to you unless you agree. I am taking notes and not recording. [Kris: adjust if recording is agreed.] You can skip any question and stop at any point. Is that all right with you?"

**Their view (5 to 12).**

1. What is your connection to this decision?
2. In your own words, what is the problem this decision is meant to solve?
3. What is working now that should not be broken?
4. What has been tried before, and what happened?

**Options and trade-offs (30 to 40).** From the second interview on, describe the candidate options neutrally, without saying which is preferred.

5. If it were your call, what would you do?
6. What would that cost, in money, time and goodwill, and who would carry the cost?
7. Here are the options I am hearing: [two or three]. What does each get right, and what does each miss?
8. What is the strongest argument against the option you prefer?
9. What would make this fail even if the right choice is made?

**Close (40 to 45).**

10. What should I have asked that I did not?
11. Who else sees this differently from you?
12. "Thank you. The paper goes to [decision owner] on [date]. [For consumers: your payment of [amount] will arrive by [date] from [payer]. You will receive a short summary of what was decided and how what you said was used.]"

## 3. Questions by sprint type (minutes 12 to 30)

Choose six to eight from the relevant set, according to who is in front of you.

### 3.1 Consumer evidence plan

Typical interviewees: the evidence owner; two or three internal users of consumer evidence (research, medical, program or policy leads); two or three consumers or community organisation leads.

1. What do you need to know about people's experience that you do not know now? What decision would it inform?
2. What consumer evidence do you hold already, and when was it last used to change something?
3. Who inside the organisation asks for it, and what do they do when it does not exist?
4. How was it collected: by whom, from whom, with what consent? Who is missing from it?
5. What would make the evidence believable to the people who have to act on it?
6. What have participants been asked to do so far, and what did it cost them in time and effort?
7. Where is it stored, who can see it, and how long is it kept?
8. If you could run one method properly in the next 12 months, which would it be, and what has stopped you?
9. For consumers: what would you want an organisation like this to understand about living with this condition that it keeps missing? How would you prefer to be asked?

Do not ask about, or discuss, any specific application to a committee. If it comes up, say it is outside this work and move on.

### 3.2 Engagement strategy

Typical interviewees: chief executive or program lead; a board member; two staff who run engagement or depend on it; two community members.

1. Why does this organisation involve its community? Where is that written, and who is accountable?
2. Who do you hear from, and who do you never hear from?
3. Tell me about the last decision that community input changed. And the last time it was asked for and made no difference.
4. What preparation do community members and staff get?
5. What are people paid, and how long does payment take?
6. What happens after people give their input? Who tells them what came of it?
7. What do you report about engagement, and to whom?
8. If you could fund one thing for the next two years, what would it be?
9. What should the organisation stop doing?
10. For community members: when have you felt your time was well used here, and when not?

These follow the seven CEOF domains in order. Use the client's diagnostic results to decide which to press.

### 3.3 Trial-readiness review

Typical interviewees: chief investigator; trial or program coordinator; two or three consumers or carers with relevant experience; a site or recruitment lead.

1. At what points have consumers shaped this study so far: the question, the outcomes, the design, the materials? What changed as a result?
2. Which outcomes matter most to the people who would take part, and are they being measured?
3. Walk me through taking part, visit by visit. Where is the burden heaviest: travel, time, procedures, time off work or school, care arrangements?
4. Who has read the participant materials from the outside? What did they not understand?
5. Who will be hard to reach or likely to withdraw, and what is planned for them?
6. How are consumers on the project team paid and supported? Is it in the budget?
7. How and when will participants hear the results?
8. What do the funder's published requirements ask for on consumer involvement, and which parts are you least confident about?
9. If a reviewer read only the consumer involvement section, what would they doubt?
10. For consumers and carers: what would make you say yes to a study like this, and what would make you say no?

This is about consumer involvement in research design and conduct. It is not scientific, regulatory or reimbursement advice.

### 3.4 Partnership approach

Typical interviewees: decision owner; a board member or senior sponsor; the relationship lead; two or three people who can speak from the other side's perspective. Do not interview the counterparty in a live negotiation unless both sides have agreed.

1. What do you want from this partnership that you cannot get alone?
2. What do you think the other side wants? How do you know?
3. What can you offer, and what will you not offer under any terms?
4. What would your community think if they read the agreement? What would they ask first?
5. Where could money, data or access to people create a problem, real or perceived?
6. Who decides, who signs, and who can stop it?
7. Tell me about a partnership that went well and one that did not. What made the difference?
8. What disclosure is required of each side, and what do you choose to disclose beyond that?
9. How would you leave the partnership if it stopped serving your community?
10. For the other side's perspective: what makes an organisation easy or hard to partner with? What do partners usually get wrong in the first approach?

For company clients, stay inside the Code clause in the proposal: no product promotion, and nothing aimed at encouraging consumers to ask for a prescription medicine.

## 4. Interviews with consumers, carers and community members

These rules apply whichever sprint type is running.

- Paid at published rates, confirmed before the invitation. Costed as a separate line in the proposal, never absorbed in the fee.
- Offer the questions in advance, a choice of time, a support person, and a phone call in place of video.
- Ask about experience and preferences. Do not ask for clinical detail that the decision does not need.
- Open with: "You are the expert on your own experience. You do not have to speak for anyone else."
- If someone becomes distressed: pause, offer a break or to stop, do not push on. Follow up by message the same day. [Kris: support contact to offer.]
- No identifying detail in notes beyond what is needed to arrange payment, which is held separately.
- Close the loop: every consumer interviewed receives a short summary after the briefing saying what was decided and how their input was used. The client's contact sends it; the handover email includes the draft.
