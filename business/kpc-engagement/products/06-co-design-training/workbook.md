## 2. What co-design is and is not

### 2.1 My definition

### 2.2 The spectrum

The IAP2 Spectrum of Public Participation, published by the International Association for Public Participation, describes five levels of involvement. Influence over the decision increases as you move down the table. The summary below is in my words.

| Level | The aim | The promise you make | A sign you are here |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Inform** | Give people balanced information so they understand the issue, the options or the decision | "You will be kept informed." | Information goes one way |
| **Consult** | Obtain people's feedback on analysis, options or decisions | "You will be listened to, and told how your input affected the decision." | You ask; you still shape the options and decide |
| **Involve** | Work with people throughout the process so their concerns and aspirations are understood and considered | "Your concerns will be reflected in the options, and you will be told how." | People are with you more than once, and what they say changes the options |
| **Collaborate** | Partner with people in each aspect of the decision, including developing the options and choosing the preferred one | "Your advice will be built into the decision as far as possible." | Options are made together |
| **Empower** | Place the final decision in people's hands | "What you decide will be carried out." | They decide; you implement |

Three things to remember:

1. No level is better in itself. The right level depends on the decision.
2. One project can hold several decisions, each at a different level.
3. The failure is promising one level and delivering a lower one.

**Where co-design sits.** Co-design means the people affected help define the problem and shape the solution together with the people who decide, and have real influence over the result. On the spectrum that is collaborate, and sometimes empower.

| Co-design is | Co-design is not |
|---|---|
| Starting before the options exist | Asking for comments on a finished draft |
| Community members and staff making something together | A survey, however well written |
| Shared influence over what is chosen | A single focus group |
| Several conversations over time | A seat at a meeting with no say |
| Paid, supported, and reported back on | A name for any activity that includes a consumer |

A survey or a focus group can be exactly the right thing to do. Call it what it is.

## 3. Choosing the level of involvement a decision warrants

### 3.1 Start with the decision

Three questions before any method is chosen:

1. What is being decided?
2. Who decides?
3. What is still open?

Then five tests for the level:

| Test | Ask | Points towards |
|---|---|---|
| Effect | How much does this decision change people's lives, care or costs? | The greater the effect, the further along the spectrum |
| Knowledge | What do community members know about this that you do not? | The more they know that you lack, the further along |
| Openness | How much can still change: the problem, the options, the choice? | Only what is open can be shared. If nothing is open, inform, and say so |
| Promise | What can you promise at this level, and can you keep it? | Choose the highest level whose promise you can keep |
| Means | Do time and budget allow this level without cutting corners on safety or pay? | If not, lower the level or change the timetable. Never cut pay or safety |

## 4. Recruiting and paying people fairly

### 4.1 Principles

**Recruiting**

- Decide whose experience the decision needs before deciding who to ask.
- Aim for a range of experience, not a statistical sample.
- Go through people and organisations the community already trusts, and give them time.
- Say plainly what is asked, how long it takes, what is paid, and what people can and cannot influence.
- Remove what gets in the way: time of day, travel, care responsibilities, technology, language, access.
- Do not ask one person to speak for everyone like them.
- Go beyond the people you always ask.

**Paying**

- Community members' time and expertise are paid, as staff time is.
- Use a published rate schedule and name it. [Kris: schedule to cite, or "your organisation's policy".]
- Pay for preparation and reading as well as the session.
- Cover costs on top of the fee: travel, care, access needs, data.
- Pay promptly, and tell people when.
- Offer a choice of how to be paid. Tell people that payments may affect tax or benefits so they can check their own situation. Do not give financial advice.
- Put it in the budget as its own line, so it is never traded against other costs.
- If someone declines payment, that is their choice. The offer is still made, every time.

## 5. Running a safe session

### 5.1 Principles

**Before**

- Tell people what will happen, who will be there and what they will be asked.
- Get informed consent in plain words, including how notes or recordings are used, stored and for how long.
- Make it clear that people can skip a question, step out or withdraw, and that their input can be removed if they ask.
- Ask about access needs and offer a support person.
- Check early whether the activity needs ethics review. Ask whoever decides that in your organisation.

**During**

- Agree how the room will work, together, at the start.
- Have a second person present whose only job is to notice how people are going.
- Ask about experience. Never require anyone to tell their story or prove what they have been through.
- Watch who speaks first, who chairs and where staff sit. Fewer staff than community members.
- Plan a break, a quiet space and an early finish, not a late one.

**When someone is distressed**

1. Pause. Do not hurry on.
2. Acknowledge what you see, briefly and without drama.
3. Offer a choice: a break, to continue, or to stop.
4. The second person steps out with them if they wish.
5. Do not try to fix it or fill the silence.
6. Follow up the same day or the next.

**After**

- Check in with everyone within a day or two.
- Give support contacts. [Kris: contacts to list.]
- Pay on time.
- Ask again before reusing anyone's words or story anywhere, each time.

## 7. Turning input into decisions

### 7.1 Four steps

1. **Record** what was said in people's own words. Do not tidy it into the answer you hoped for.
2. **Sort** into themes. Keep the outliers; one person's point can be the one that matters most.
3. **Check** the themes with participants before acting: "This is what I heard. Is it right?"
4. **Decide**, and keep a record for each theme: what was said, what was decided, why, and who decided.

Some input will not be taken up. That is legitimate when the reason is stated honestly. Choosing only the quotes that support a decision already made is not.

### 7.2 Input-to-decision table

In the room, fill this in for one project at your table using input you imagine people might give. This is practice only. For your real session, use only what people said.

| Theme | What people said, in their words | How many raised it | What was decided | Why | Who decided | What changes, and by when |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | Taken up in full | | | |
| | | | Taken up in part | | | |
| | | | Not taken up | | | |

Test for the "not taken up" row: if it were your input, would you accept that reason?

Reasons that hold: a fixed legal, safety or funding requirement; cost, with the figure; a direct conflict with what other participants said, with how it was weighed. Reasons that do not: "out of scope", "not feasible", "noted".

## 8. Closing the loop

### 8.1 What it means

Tell the people who took part:

- what was heard;
- what was decided;
- what changed because of them;
- what was not taken up, and why;
- what happens next, and whether they can stay involved.

Do it twice: a short note within two weeks of the session, and again when the decision is made. Use the channel each person prefers. Ask how the experience was for them.

### 8.2 My draft message

Write it as if your session has happened. Leave gaps where you cannot know yet.

> Dear [name],
>
> Thank you for taking part in [session] on [date].
>
> **What I heard.** [Two or three themes, in participants' words.]
>
> **What has been decided.** [The decision, who made it, when.]
>
> **What changed because of you.** [One or two specific changes.]
>
> **What was not taken up, and why.** [Be specific.]
>
> **What happens next.** [Next step and date. How to stay involved, if they wish.]
>
> **Your payment.** [Amount, date paid or due.]
>
> If I have misunderstood anything you said, please tell me. I would also like to know how the session was for you: [one question or a link].
>
> [Name, role, contact]

| | |
|---|---|
| First message (what was heard) sent by | [date], by [who] |
| Second message (what was decided) sent by | [date], by [who] |

### 8.3 Checklist: closing the loop

- [ ] A named person owns it.
- [ ] The first message goes within two weeks of the session.
- [ ] Participants have checked that the themes are right.
- [ ] The second message goes when the decision is made, with a date set now.
- [ ] It says what changed because of their input, specifically.
- [ ] It says what was not taken up, and why.
- [ ] It is in plain language and in the format each person prefers.
- [ ] Everyone has been paid before or with the message.
- [ ] People are asked how the experience was, and the answers are read.
- [ ] People are told how to stay involved, or that this is the end, plainly.
- [ ] The people who helped recruit are told the outcome too.
- [ ] The record of what was said, decided and why is kept where the next project lead can find it.
