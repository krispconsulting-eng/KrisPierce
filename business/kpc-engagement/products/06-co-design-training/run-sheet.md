# In-house co-design training: run sheet

Internal. The full day, 9:00 to 4:30. Workbook page references are to `workbook.md` sections. Materials quantities are in `PRODUCT.md` section 9.

## The design of the day

- Every participant works on **one real upcoming project of their own**, named in the pre-work. Each session teaches one thing and then participants apply it to that project in the workbook. By 2:30 the pieces add up to a one-page session plan.
- Kris talks for no more than 15 minutes in any session. The rest is participants working.
- Written work before discussion, so quieter people have something to say.
- The spectrum used all day is the IAP2 Spectrum of Public Participation (International Association for Public Participation): inform, consult, involve, collaborate, empower.

## 9:20 to 10:05. Session 1. What co-design is and is not

**Purpose:** a shared, accurate vocabulary. Participants can place any activity on the spectrum.

| Time | Activity | Materials |
|---|---|---|
| 9:20 | Individual, 2 minutes: write your own one-sentence definition of co-design (workbook 2.1). Hear four or five. Note how much they differ. | Workbook 2.1 |
| 9:25 | Kris input, 10 minutes: the IAP2 spectrum. Walk along the five wall cards. For each level: the aim, and the promise it makes to the people involved. Inform: give people balanced information so they understand; the promise is to keep them informed. Consult: obtain feedback on analysis, options or decisions; the promise is to listen and report back how their input affected the decision. Involve: work with people throughout so their concerns and aspirations are understood and considered; the promise is that those concerns are reflected in the options developed. Collaborate: partner with people in each aspect of the decision, including developing options and choosing the preferred one; the promise is to build their advice into the decision as far as possible. Empower: place the final decision in their hands; the promise is to carry out what they decide. Three points to make: influence over the decision increases from left to right; no level is better in itself, the right one depends on the decision; the failure is promising one level and delivering a lower one. | Wall cards, slide |
| 9:35 | Kris input, 3 minutes: where co-design sits. Co-design means the people affected help define the problem and shape the solution with decision-makers, with real influence over the result. On the spectrum that is collaborate, and sometimes empower. A survey is not co-design. A focus group on a finished draft is not co-design. Both can be the right thing to do; call them what they are. | Workbook 2.2 |
| 9:38 | Table activity, 12 minutes: the spectrum sort. Each table gets 12 activity cards and places each under a wall card with a sticky note. Tables must agree. | Activity cards, sticky notes |
| 9:50 | Debrief at the wall, 8 minutes: go to the cards where tables disagree. Ask: "What promise did this activity make? Who held the decision at the end?" Draw out that the name of an activity (advisory group, workshop) does not settle its level; who holds influence does. | |
| 9:58 | Individual, 5 minutes: where is my project now, and what was it going to be called? (workbook 2.3). Two volunteers share. | Workbook 2.3 |
| 10:03 | Bridge: "You have said where it is. Next: where should it be?" | |

**Facilitator notes:** card 6 (an advisory group that only hears updates) and card 11 (quick feedback the day before printing) cause the best arguments; allow them. Do not let "empower" be read as the goal for everything. If someone says "we always co-design", ask which decision the community last changed.

Activity cards and suggested placements are in workbook 2.2 (cards) and at the end of this run sheet (answers).

## 10:05 to 10:45. Session 2. Choosing the level of involvement a decision warrants

**Purpose:** each participant names the decision in their project and chooses a level, with a reason.

| Time | Activity | Materials |
|---|---|---|
| 10:05 | Kris input, 8 minutes: start with the decision, not the method. Three questions: What is being decided? Who decides? What is still open? Then five tests for the level: how much the decision affects people's lives; how much they know that you do not; how much is truly open to change; what you are able to promise and keep; what time and money allow without cutting corners on safety or pay. If nothing is open, the honest level is inform, and that is acceptable. | Slide, workbook 3.1 |
| 10:13 | Individual, 10 minutes: complete the decision canvas (workbook 3.2): the decision, who decides, what is fixed, what is open, who is affected, the level chosen and the promise that goes with it. | Workbook 3.2 |
| 10:23 | Pairs, 12 minutes (6 each): explain your canvas. Partner asks the three challenge questions printed in workbook 3.3: "What could the community change?" "What have you already decided without saying so?" "Can you keep the promise at that level?" | Workbook 3.3 |
| 10:35 | Whole room, 8 minutes: each person places a dot on the wall spectrum for where their project should be. Look at the spread. Ask two people who moved their level in the last 20 minutes to say why. | Dots |
| 10:43 | Bridge to the break: "After the break: who needs to be in the room, and what you owe them." | |

**Facilitator notes:** the common error is choosing collaborate when most of the project is already fixed. Praise anyone who moves down to consult honestly. The second common error is one level for the whole project; show that one project can hold several decisions at different levels.

## 11:00 to 12:00. Session 3. Recruiting and paying people fairly

**Purpose:** each participant has a recruitment plan that reaches past the usual people, and a costed payment line.

| Time | Activity | Materials |
|---|---|---|
| 11:00 | Table discussion, 5 minutes: "Who do you always ask? Who have you never heard from?" One answer per table to the room. | |
| 11:05 | Kris input, 12 minutes. Recruiting: decide whose experience the decision needs before deciding who to ask; look for range of experience, not a statistical sample; go through people and organisations the community already trusts; say plainly what is asked, how long, what is paid and what the person can and cannot influence; remove barriers (time of day, travel, care, technology, language, access); do not ask one person to speak for everyone. Paying: people's time and expertise are paid, as staff time is. Use a published rate schedule and name it. Pay for preparation and reading time as well as the session. Cover costs on top: travel, care, access, data. Pay promptly and say when. Offer a choice of method and tell people that payments may affect tax or benefits so they can check for themselves; do not give financial advice. Budget it as its own line so it is never traded against other costs. A gift card in place of a fee, chosen for the organisation's convenience, is not payment. | Slide, workbook 4.1 |
| 11:17 | Individual, 15 minutes: complete the recruitment and payment canvas (workbook 4.2): whose experience, how many, where they will be found, what will get in their way, the ask in two sentences, the rate, the total. | Workbook 4.2 |
| 11:32 | Tables, 13 minutes: each person reads their two-sentence ask aloud. The table answers as a busy carer would: "Would I say yes? What would I want to know?" Rewrite the ask. | Workbook 4.3 |
| 11:45 | Whole room, 10 minutes: totals. Each person says their payment total aloud; Kris writes them on a flip chart. Discuss: who approves this, and from which budget? The person in the room who holds a budget answers. | Flip chart |
| 11:55 | Checklist walk-through, 5 minutes: recruitment and payment checklist (workbook 4.4). Tick what your project already has. | Workbook 4.4 |

**Facilitator notes:** do not quote a dollar rate from memory. Point to the organisation's own policy if it has one, or to the published schedule named in the workbook. [Kris: schedule.] If the organisation has no policy, say so plainly and add "payment policy" to the flip chart for the sponsor at 4:00. Expect the objection "we have no budget"; the answer is that the cost exists either way, and today it is being made visible. For company participants: payments to consumers follow their Code obligations and written agreements; stay general.

## 12:45 to 1:45. Session 4. Running a safe session

**Purpose:** participants can plan for safety and consent, and have practised responding when something goes wrong.

| Time | Activity | Materials |
|---|---|---|
| 12:45 | Individual then tables, 7 minutes: "Think of a meeting where you felt you could not say what you thought. What made it so?" Write three things; share at tables. Kris collects on a flip chart: these are what to design out. | Flip chart |
| 12:52 | Kris input, 13 minutes. Before: tell people what will happen and what will be asked; get informed consent in plain words, including how notes or recordings are used and stored; make clear they can skip a question, step out or withdraw; ask about access needs; offer a support person; check whether the activity needs ethics review. During: an agreement for the room; a second person present whose only job is to watch how people are going; ask about experience, not for proof of suffering; never require someone to tell their story; a quiet space and a break plan. When someone is distressed: pause, acknowledge, offer a choice (break, continue, stop), do not hurry on and do not try to fix it; the second person steps out with them if they wish. After: check in the same or next day; give support contacts; pay on time; ask again before reusing anyone's words or story. Power: who chairs, who speaks first, where people sit, what staff wear and say. | Slide, workbook 5.1 |
| 1:05 | Practice in threes, 25 minutes: scenario cards (workbook 5.2). Roles: facilitator, participant, observer. Four scenarios, 5 minutes each with 1 minute of feedback from the observer; rotate roles. Scenarios: a participant becomes tearful while describing a diagnosis; one person dominates and speaks for "all families"; a participant challenges whether anything will change; a staff member corrects a participant's account of their own care. | Scenario cards |
| 1:30 | Debrief, 8 minutes: "What did you say that worked? What would you not say again?" Collect useful phrases on a flip chart. | Flip chart |
| 1:38 | Individual, 7 minutes: safety and consent checklist for your project (workbook 5.3). Mark the three items you do not yet have. | Workbook 5.3 |

**Facilitator notes:** say before the practice that people may play a role lightly and may sit out; some participants will be close to these experiences. Watch the room. Use the quiet space if needed and model the response being taught. Do not take questions about how a particular ethics committee will rule; the teaching point is to ask early. Keep examples general; no real patient stories.

## 2:45 to 3:30. Session 6. Turning input into decisions

**Purpose:** participants can get from what people said to a decision, and show the trail.

| Time | Activity | Materials |
|---|---|---|
| 2:45 | Kris input, 10 minutes: four steps. Record what was said in people's own words, without tidying it into your preferred answer. Sort into themes, and keep the outliers: one person's point can be the most important one. Test themes with participants before acting ("this is what I heard; is it right?"). Then decide, with a record for each theme: what was said, what was decided, why, who decided. Some input will not be taken up. That is legitimate when the reason is stated. What is not legitimate: selecting the quotes that support a decision already made. | Slide, workbook 7.1 |
| 2:55 | Table activity, 20 minutes: each table takes one member's project. The project owner states the decision. The table writes ten things community members might plausibly say (on sticky notes), sorts them into themes, and fills three rows of the input-to-decision table (workbook 7.2), including one row where the input is not taken up and the reason is given. | Sticky notes, workbook 7.2 |
| 3:15 | Whole room, 10 minutes: each table reads out its "not taken up" row. Room tests the reason: "Would you accept that, if it were your input?" | |
| 3:25 | Individual, 5 minutes: on your session plan, complete "how input becomes a decision": who sorts it, who checks it with participants, who decides, by when (workbook 6.2). | Workbook 6.2 |

**Facilitator notes:** be clear that the sticky notes are imagined input for practice only, and that nothing invented goes into a real report. The learning is in the "not taken up" row; push for reasons that are specific (cost, a fixed requirement, a conflict with other input) and not "out of scope".

**Purpose:** each participant has drafted the message they will send back, and set its date.

| Time | Activity | Materials |
|---|---|---|
| 3:30 | Table discussion, 4 minutes: "When did you last give feedback and never hear what happened? What did you conclude?" | |
| 3:34 | Kris input, 6 minutes: closing the loop means telling the people who took part what was heard, what was decided, what changed because of them, what was not taken up and why, and what happens next. ## Spectrum sort: suggested placements

Placements are a basis for discussion, not a marking key. The test is who held influence over the decision.

| Card | Activity | Suggested level | Point to draw out |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A newsletter article announcing a new service | Inform | One-way; useful and honest |
| 2 | A survey on a draft strategy, with results summarised for the board | Consult | Only if someone reports back how input affected the result |
| 3 | A public webinar explaining research results | Inform | Questions at the end do not change the level |
| 4 | One focus group reacting to a leaflet that is already designed | Consult | Little is open; say so |
| 5 | A series of workshops through a project, with a record of each concern raised and how it was dealt with | Involve | Sustained, and concerns shape the options |
| 6 | A consumer advisory group that meets quarterly to hear updates | Inform | The name suggests more than the practice delivers |
| 7 | Carers and staff develop the options for a new support program together and agree the preferred one | Collaborate | This is what co-design usually looks like |
| 8 | Two consumers on a research team as investigators, with a say in design and budget | Collaborate | Depends on whether the say is real |
| 9 | A community panel decides which small grants are funded, and the organisation funds them | Empower | The decision is in their hands and is carried out |
| 10 | A community steering group sets a program's priorities and the organisation implements them | Empower | Only if the organisation cannot overrule |
| 11 | Emailing three known families for quick feedback the day before printing | Consult at most | Nothing is open; closer to inform |
| 12 | Interviews early in a project to understand experience, with themes checked back and used to choose what is measured | Involve | Early and used, though people did not shape the options together |

## If time runs short

- Session 1: sort eight cards, not 12.
- Session 4: three scenarios, not four.
- Session 6: two rows of the table, one of them "not taken up".
- Never cut: the 25 minutes on the session plan, the payment totals, or the sponsor's response at 4:15.
