# Synthesis method

> **What this is:** how raw input from interviews and co-design sessions becomes findings, and how findings become decisions. The same method every time, so a client can see how a sentence said in a session ended up as a line in their framework.

## Principles

1. Every finding traces to what someone said. Every recommendation traces to a finding.
2. A view held by one person can matter more than a view held by seven. Count, and then weigh.
3. I report what was said. The organisation decides what it will do. The two are kept apart on the page.
4. Participants check the summary before it is final.
5. Nothing in the output identifies a person.

## Step 1: Build the raw input log

Within 2 working days of each interview or session, enter every distinct point as one row. One idea per row. Use the speaker's words where possible.

| ID | Source | Group | Participant code | What was said (close to verbatim) | Activity | Quote consent |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R001 | Session 1 | A | P3 | [text] | Moments that mattered | Yes or no |
| R002 | Interview | Staff | S2 | [text] | Question 3 | Not applicable |

Rules:
- Participant codes only. The key linking codes to names is stored separately and deleted on the date promised in the information sheet.
- Staff and consumer input are kept in the same log with different source tags, so differences between them can be seen.
- If a participant withdraws a contribution, delete the row.
- For company clients, the log never leaves Kris Pierce Consulting.

## Step 2: Code each row

Two passes.

**Pass 1: domain code.** Tag each row with one of the seven CEOF domains (a second where a row sits across two):

1. Purpose and Governance
2. Voice and Representation
3. Capability and Culture
4. Engagement Practice
5. Safety, Ethics and Recognition
6. Feedback and Learning
7. Outcomes and Impact

**Pass 2: open code.** Give each row a short label in plain words for what it is about, taken from the data, not decided in advance (for example: "asked too late", "paid slowly", "never heard back"). Aim for 15 to 30 open codes in total. Merge codes that mean the same thing; split any code holding more than about a fifth of all rows.

**Also tag each row:**
- Type: experience, cause, idea, condition ("I would take part if"), or warning.
- Direction: what works, what does not, what is wanted.

**Check:** after coding, re-read one in five rows cold. If the code no longer fits, fix the code definition and re-code. For Full, I ask the second person from the sessions to read the code list against their own memory of the sessions and tell me what is missing. ## Step 3: Group codes into themes

A theme is a statement, not a topic. "Payment" is a topic. "People are paid so late that taking part costs them money" is a theme.

For each theme record: the statement; the open codes it draws on; the number of rows; the number of distinct participants; which groups raised it; whether staff and consumers see it the same way.

## Step 4: Weigh, including minority views

Frequency is one input. Each theme is weighed on four things:

| Test | Question | Scale |
|---|---|---|
| Breadth | How many distinct participants raised it, and across how many groups? | Few, some, most |
| Depth | How serious is the consequence for the people affected? | Inconvenience, barrier, harm |
| Who carries it | Is it raised by, or about, people who are usually missing from this organisation's engagement? | Yes or no |
| Bearing on the decision | Would acting on it change the decision this project serves? | Directly, indirectly, not at all |

**Minority view rule.** A point raised by one or two people is carried forward as a finding in its own right, not folded into a larger theme, if any of these is true:

- the consequence is a barrier or harm;
- it comes from, or concerns, a group the recruitment brief named as under-heard;
- it contradicts a majority theme (the disagreement is itself a finding);
- it was marked "protect" in session 2.

**What I do not do:**
- Resolve a disagreement by averaging. Both positions are reported, with who holds each.
- Drop a point because only one person said it.
- Report a count as if it were a survey. With 6 to 16 participants, "most" and "some" are honest; percentages are not.
- Let the loudest session dominate. For Full, each group's themes are built separately before they are compared.

**Minority views register** (kept alongside the findings table):

| ID | View | Raised by (codes) | Why it is carried forward | How the framework answers it | If not answered, why |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| M01 | [text] | P5 | Harm; under-heard group | [section and action] | [reason] |

## Step 5: Write the findings table

One row per finding. Up to 10 for Focused, 15 for Standard, 20 for Full.

### Findings table template

| No. | Finding (one sentence, a statement) | CEOF domain | Evidence (row IDs; breadth) | Weight (breadth, depth, who carries it, bearing) | Minority or contested? | What it means for the decision | Recommended response | Organisation's decision (accept, accept in part, decline) | Reason if declined or partial | Framework section |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| F01 | [finding] | [domain name, exactly as listed] | [R003, R017, R022; most participants, both groups] | [most; barrier; yes; directly] | [no / minority, see M01 / contested: group A and group B differ] | [text] | [text] | [filled at decision point 3] | [text] | [for example: 6. Payment and recognition] |

Writing rules for findings:
- A finding says what is the case, in plain words, and could be disagreed with.
- No participant is identifiable. No quote without consent.
- The "recommended response" column is mine. The "organisation's decision" column is the client's. Never fill the client's column for them.
- For Full, add a column "Group A, Group B" showing where the groups agreed and differed.

## Step 6: Decide (decision point 3)

In a 60 to 90 minute meeting with the decision owner, go down the findings table:

1. Accept, accept in part, or decline. One of the three for every row; "noted" is not an option.
2. For every "decline" and "in part", a reason the organisation is willing to say to participants in writing.
3. For every "accept", an owner and a date. These go into the framework's first-year plan.
4. Every row in the minority views register gets an answer.

If a reason cannot be said to participants, it is not yet a reason. Work on it until it can.

## Step 7: Check with participants

Send the draft report back to every participant. One paid hour each. Two questions only:

1. Have I heard you correctly? What is wrong or missing?
2. Is anything here that could identify you?

Corrections are made. Disagreement with the organisation's decisions is recorded and passed on; it does not hold up the report.

## Step 8: Report back

The report back goes to every participant no later than the day the client receives the final framework. It is at most three pages, in plain language. It is the organisation's document: the commitments in it are the organisation's, so it is written in the organisation's voice and signed by the decision owner. I draft it and they own it.

Checks before it is sent:
- [ ] Every finding in the findings table appears, in plain words.
- [ ] Every "will not" has a reason a participant would recognise as a reason.
- [ ] No names, no identifying detail, no quotes without consent.
- [ ] No product names (company clients).
- [ ] Reading level about Year 8. No acronyms unexplained.
- [ ] Sent by the route each participant chose, with an offer of a phone call.

## What goes to the client, and what does not

| Item | Client receives | Company client receives |
|---|---|---|
| Findings table | Yes | Yes, themes only, no counts for groups under ten |
| Minority views register | Yes, coded | Yes, coded, with any identifying detail removed |
| Report back | Yes | Yes |
| Raw input log | On request, coded, after a re-identification check | No |
| Session notes | Summary only | No |
| Recordings, names, contact details | No | No |
