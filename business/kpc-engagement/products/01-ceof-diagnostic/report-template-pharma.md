# Patient Engagement Review: written report template (pharmaceutical)

> **What this is:** the report emailed after a company completes the Patient Engagement Review, as a fill-in template. Use it to write a report by hand, or to check what the automated writer produces (`report-prompt-pharma.md`).
> **How to use it:** keep one level paragraph, one paragraph per domain (chosen by score band), and the first-quarter action for each of the three lowest domains. Delete the rest and every quoted block. A finished report runs 900 to 1,200 words.
> **Rules:** domain and level names exactly as in `../CEOF-REFERENCE.md`; level meanings and first moves exactly as in `AUDIENCE-VERSIONS.md`. Never comment on a product, a submission or a committee. Nothing is sent without Kris's approval.

**Subject:** Your Patient Engagement Review report

Hi [first name],

Thank you for completing the Patient Engagement Review for [organisation]. This report sets out where your engagement stands, what each of the seven domains shows, your three priorities and what to do next. It rests on [one person's / number of people's] answers, so read it as a starting point for a conversation inside the affiliate.

## 1. Where you stand

**Overall result:** [overall] out of 5. Level [number]: [level name].

> Keep one paragraph. The level is the overall score rounded to the nearest whole number.

**Level 1, Ad hoc (1.0 to 1.4).** Patient engagement happens when a project needs it. There is goodwill, and individuals who do it well, but nothing holds it together between projects. Treat this as a starting line, not a verdict: most companies begin here. The first gains come from naming an owner and a purpose, well before adding activity.

**Level 2, Emerging (1.5 to 2.4).** There is real activity, but it sits in pockets and rarely reaches decisions. Each function has its own contacts, its own habits and its own view of what engagement is for. This is a starting line, not a verdict. The next step is to connect what already happens to two or three decisions that matter this year.

**Level 3, Structured (2.5 to 3.4).** Reliable mechanisms exist, but patient input does not yet shape plans. You can list your activities with confidence. It is harder to say which protocol, evidence plan or support program changed because of them. The step up is about timing and authority: input that arrives while a decision is still open, in front of the people who make it.

**Level 4, Embedded (3.5 to 4.4).** Patient input is part of how development, evidence and support decisions get made. Leaders ask for it, plans allow for it, and patient organisations can see where it went. The work now is consistency across functions, and testing your own view against what patient organisations say about working with you.

**Level 5, Community-led (4.5 to 5.0).** Patients and patient organisations share in setting priorities and judging results. Few companies reach this. The work now is keeping it honest as people, portfolios and budgets change, and checking that a second person in the affiliate would score it the same way.

## 2. Domain by domain

> For each domain keep the paragraph that matches its score: low 1.0 to 2.4, middle 2.5 to 3.4, high 3.5 to 5.0.

### Purpose and Governance: [score]

**Low.** Patient engagement has no single home yet. There is no written strategy naming the decisions it must inform, no one leader accountable across the affiliate, and no budget of its own. The practical cost is that medical, market access, commercial and corporate affairs each approach patient organisations in their own way, and those organisations notice.

**Middle.** The foundations are partly there: a strategy or an accountable leader exists, and the leadership team sees engagement from time to time. What is missing is the link to decisions and a clear division of work between functions. Until the strategy names the decisions it must inform, reviews will report activity.

**High.** Engagement is governed as a capability: a written strategy, one accountable leader, a scheduled leadership review and a budget. The functions know who does what. Keep the strategy tied to this year's decisions, and ask patient organisations whether the coordination looks as tidy from outside.

### Voice and Representation: [score]

**Low.** You hear from patients mainly through one-off advisory boards, and mostly from the people and organisations you already know. Carers, and people outside the better-resourced patient organisations, are largely absent. Local input rarely reaches global teams in time to change anything.

**Middle.** There is a regular way to hear from patients and carers, but its reach is narrow or its timing is late. Patient insight is sometimes in the room when trial design, evidence plans and support programs are decided. The test is whether you can show when local input changed a global decision.

**High.** A standing panel or council gives you a range of voices, including carers and people beyond the usual organisations, and their insight is present when plans are decided. Keep renewing who is at the table, and keep a record of where local input changed a global plan.

### Capability and Culture: [score]

**Low.** Staff who meet patients are trained in compliance and left to work out engagement practice for themselves. Patients and carers are asked into roles with little briefing or support. Engagement is added to medical, access and brand plans late, if at all, so it competes for time and money that are already spent.

**Middle.** Some teams do this well, and it shows in how they brief people and plan ahead. It is not yet consistent. Training still leans towards what staff must not do, and leaders do not routinely ask what patients said before they approve a plan.

**High.** Staff are trained in engagement practice as well as compliance, patients and carers are properly briefed and supported, and leaders ask what patients said before they sign off. Engagement is resourced in plans from the start. Protect this when teams change, because it lives in people.

### Engagement Practice: [score]

**Low.** Patients are involved near launch or reimbursement, when most decisions are already made. Activities are chosen before anyone has named the decision they serve, and the method is usually whatever was used last time. People take part on the company's terms: its timing, its format, its language.

**Middle.** You plan engagement with some care, and the method often fits the question. Involvement still starts later than it should, and not every activity can be traced to a decision. Where your presence might shape the answers, independent facilitation is not yet routine.

**High.** Patients are involved from early development and trial design. Each activity serves a named decision at a level of involvement that fits it, with independent facilitation where needed, and participation is designed around people's lives. Keep asking whether the decision was still open when you asked.

### Safety, Ethics and Recognition: [score]

**Low.** Payment to patients and carers varies by function and by who asks, and costs are slow to be reimbursed. Staff are unsure where the lines sit under the Medicines Australia Code of Conduct, so they either hold back or take risks. The independence of patient organisations you fund is assumed without being written down.

**Middle.** The basics are in place: engagement is non-promotional, consent and adverse event reporting are handled, and people are paid. The gaps are consistency and transparency. One written payment policy, applied the same way in every function, and clear independence principles would close most of them.

**High.** You pay fair market value under one written policy, reimburse promptly, work inside the Code with confidence, and fund patient organisations in ways that keep them independent. This is a strength to build on. Publishing your principles, if you have not, lets patient organisations hold you to them.

### Feedback and Learning: [score]

**Low.** Patients and patient organisations give input and rarely hear what happened to it. Insight stays with the team that collected it, so the same people are asked the same questions by different functions. Nobody asks patient organisations what you are like to work with.

**Middle.** Feedback happens, but it depends on the individual running the activity. Insight is recorded in some places and shared in fewer. You review your approach, though the review does not yet draw on what patient organisations say about the experience of working with you.

**High.** You close the loop as a matter of course, including on what you did not act on and why. Insight is shared across functions, patient organisations tell you how you are doing, and you change in response. Keep the annual review honest by inviting one outside view into it.

### Outcomes and Impact: [score]

**Low.** You can count activities, but you cannot yet point to a protocol, an endpoint, a support program or an evidence plan that changed because of patient input. Reporting stops at what the Code requires. Patients have not been asked what outcomes matter to them, or what good partnership looks like.

**Middle.** You have examples of decisions that changed, though they are held as stories and are hard to find when asked. Some outcomes that matter to patients are measured. Reporting back to patient organisations goes little further than transparency reporting.

**High.** You can show decisions that changed, you measure outcomes patients told you matter alongside clinical and commercial measures, and you report to the patient organisations involved beyond what the Code requires. They help define good partnership and judge it. Few companies can say this; keep the evidence current.

## 3. Your three priorities

Your three lowest domains are your priorities: [domain], [domain] and [domain]. For each, here is a first move and what it looks like across your first quarter.

> Keep the three that apply. The first sentence of each is the first move, word for word.

**Purpose and Governance.** Write one page for the leadership team: what patient engagement is for here, who is accountable, and the three decisions this year it must inform. Month one: draft it with the heads of medical, market access, commercial and corporate affairs. Month two: take it to the leadership team for a named owner and a review date. Month three: agree one contact route for each patient organisation.

**Voice and Representation.** Set up one standing patient and carer panel with terms of reference, and put one real decision in front of it this quarter. Month one: write the terms, covering purpose, term, payment at fair market value and how advice is used. Month two: recruit beyond the organisations you already know, and include carers. Month three: hold the first meeting on a decision that is still open.

**Capability and Culture.** Run one session for every team that meets patients: engagement practice first, compliance second. Month one: list those teams and ask what they find hardest. Month two: run the session, built on real situations from your own work. Month three: add a short briefing for patients and carers taking on a role, and one line on engagement to plan sign-off.

**Engagement Practice.** Add one line to every plan template: what decision is this, and when did patients inform it? Month one: add the line to medical, access and brand plan templates. Month two: test it on one plan in each function and note where the answer is "too late". Month three: move one activity earlier as a result, and decide where independent facilitation is needed.

**Safety, Ethics and Recognition.** Write down your payment and independence principles for patients and patient organisations, publish them, and apply them the same way in every function. Month one: collect what each function pays and how long payment takes. Month two: agree one policy with compliance and finance. Month three: publish the principles and tell the patient organisations you work with.

**Feedback and Learning.** Close one loop this month: go back to the last advisory board and tell its members what changed because of them. Month one: send that note, including what you did not act on and why. Month two: make the note a standard step after every activity. Month three: set up one shared record of insight that all functions can see, with consent built in.

**Outcomes and Impact.** Ask three patient organisations one question: what would tell you we are a good partner? Start measuring it. Month one: ask, and listen without defending. Month two: choose two or three measures from what you heard. Month three: collect the first results, share them with those organisations, and record one decision that changed because of patient input.

## 4. What to do next

1. Share this report with the people who own your next planning cycle, and ask which result an outsider would notice first.
2. Ask a colleague in another function to take the review separately, then compare. Where your results differ by a point or more is where to talk first.
3. Start one first move within two weeks, with a named owner.
4. Retake the review in 12 months.
