# Community Engagement Review: written report template (charity)

> **What this is:** the report emailed after a charity or community organisation completes the Community Engagement Review, as a fill-in template. Use it to write a report by hand, or to check what the automated writer produces (`report-prompt-charity.md`).
> **How to use it:** keep one level paragraph, one paragraph per domain (chosen by score band), and the first-quarter action for each of the three lowest domains. Delete the rest and every quoted block. A finished report runs 900 to 1,200 words.
> **Rules:** domain names, level names, level meanings and first moves exactly as in `../CEOF-REFERENCE.md`. Written for small teams with limited time: every suggestion must be something a part-time coordinator or a volunteer board could do. Nothing is sent without Kris's approval.

**Subject:** Your Community Engagement Review report

Hi [first name],

Thank you for completing the Community Engagement Review for [organisation]. This report sets out where you stand, what each of the seven domains shows, your three priorities and what to do next. I have kept every suggestion small enough for a team with little time to spare.

## 1. Where you stand

**Overall result:** [overall] out of 5. Level [number]: [level name].

> Keep one paragraph. The level is the overall score rounded to the nearest whole number.

**Level 1, Ad hoc (1.0 to 1.4).** Engagement happens when someone thinks of it. That usually means it rests on one or two committed people and the relationships they hold. A low score is a starting line, not a verdict: most organisations begin here. One written reason and one named person will do more than any new activity.

**Level 2, Emerging (1.5 to 2.4).** There is genuine activity, and it is disconnected from decisions. You ask your community things, and you care about the answers, but the answers do not yet reach the board table or the program plan in time. This is a starting line, not a verdict. The next step is to attach what you already do to one real decision.

**Level 3, Structured (2.5 to 3.4).** Reliable mechanisms exist, but involvement does not yet shape plans. You have a group, a survey or a regular forum that people can count on. The step up is to put open decisions in front of them, early enough for the plan to change.

**Level 4, Embedded (3.5 to 4.4).** Community involvement is part of how decisions get made. People in your community can point to things that are different because of them. The work now is to make sure it does not depend on the current staff and board, and to widen who is at the table.

**Level 5, Community-led (4.5 to 5.0).** Your community shares power over direction, priorities and resources. Few organisations reach this. The work now is keeping it honest as people and funding change, and checking that a second person would score it the same way.

## 2. Domain by domain

> For each domain keep the paragraph that matches its score: low 1.0 to 2.4, middle 2.5 to 3.4, high 3.5 to 5.0.

### Purpose and Governance: [score]

**Low.** Nobody has written down why you involve your community or what you want it to change, and no one on the board or leadership team is named as accountable. Engagement comes up at board meetings when something has gone wrong. With no time or money set aside, it survives on goodwill.

**Middle.** There is a purpose, or a named person, but not yet both working together. The board hears about engagement now and then without it being a standing item. A small, protected amount of time and money would make what you already do much steadier.

**High.** You have a written purpose, a named person accountable, a standing place on the board agenda and resources set aside. That is a strong base. Read the purpose statement aloud once a year and ask whether it still describes what you want to change.

### Voice and Representation: [score]

**Low.** You hear from the people who are closest to you, and often from the same few. There is no standing group with terms of reference, and community members are not at the table where decisions are made. The people who are harder to reach are not being heard at all.

**Middle.** A group or panel exists and people know how to reach you. Its membership is narrower than your community, or it advises without any say in what is decided. Renewing who is involved, even one or two new people a year, would spread the load.

**High.** Community members have a standing way to advise you, they reflect the range of your community, and they sit where decisions are made with a real say. You also renew who is at the table. Keep watching for who is missing as your community changes.

### Capability and Culture: [score]

**Low.** Community members are asked into roles without an induction, and staff and volunteers have not been briefed on working with them. Leaders approve plans without asking who from the community was involved. Engagement sits on top of full workloads, so it is the first thing to slip.

**Middle.** Some preparation happens, mostly because one person makes sure of it. It is not yet a habit across the organisation. A short induction and a single briefing for staff and volunteers would make good practice less dependent on who is in the room.

**High.** Community members are inducted and supported, staff and volunteers are briefed, and leaders ask who has been involved before they approve a plan. Engagement is written into roles. Hold on to this when people leave by keeping the induction written down.

### Engagement Practice: [score]

**Low.** Engagement is planned after the project is, so people are asked once the plan is nearly fixed. The method is usually the one you used last time, often a survey. Timing, format and technology suit the organisation more than the people taking part.

**Middle.** You think about how and when to involve people, and sometimes they are in early enough to change the plan. It is not yet decided project by project. Asking one question at the start of each project would make this consistent.

**High.** For each project you decide what involvement the decision warrants, involve people early, choose a method that fits the question and design it around the people taking part. That is skilled practice. Share how you do it, because other organisations will ask.

### Safety, Ethics and Recognition: [score]

**Low.** Community members give their time without payment or recognition under any written policy. Consent is informal, and people may not know how their stories will be used. There is no plan for when taking part is distressing, and funder or industry influence is not discussed openly.

**Middle.** You recognise people's time in some way and you are careful with their stories. What is missing is a written policy and a plan that everyone knows. Without them, how people are treated depends on who is running the activity.

**High.** You pay or recognise people under a written policy, they give informed consent, there is a plan for distress, and you manage conflicts of interest and funder influence openly. This protects your community and your independence. Publish the policy if you have not.

### Feedback and Learning: [score]

**Low.** People tell you things and do not hear what happened next. What you learn is held in someone's head or inbox, so each new project starts from nothing. Nobody asks community members how the engagement itself went.

**Middle.** You go back to people some of the time, usually when the news is good. A record of what you have heard exists in part. Telling people what you did not act on, and why, is the next habit to build.

**High.** You tell people what happened to their input, including what you did not act on. You ask how the engagement went, keep a record that new staff can start from, and review your approach each year. This is what earns trust for the next request.

### Outcomes and Impact: [score]

**Low.** You can say what you did, but you cannot yet point to a decision that changed because of community input. Reports to the board and funders count activity. Your community has not been asked what success looks like to them.

**Middle.** You have one or two good examples of decisions that changed, and some of what you measure came from your community. Reporting still leans on numbers of sessions and people. Funders increasingly ask for the other kind of evidence.

**High.** You can show decisions that changed, you measure outcomes your community told you matter, and you report on them to your board, funders and community. Community members help decide what success looks like. Keep the examples in one place so they are ready when asked for.

## 3. Your three priorities

Your three lowest domains are your priorities: [domain], [domain] and [domain]. For each, here is a first move and what it looks like across your first quarter. None needs more than a few hours a month.

> Keep the three that apply. The first sentence of each is the first move, word for word.

**Purpose and Governance.** Take a one-page proposal to the next board meeting: why the organisation involves its community, who is accountable, and what it needs. Month one: draft the page with one board member and one community member. Month two: take it to the board and ask for a named person and a standing agenda item. Month three: agree a small amount of time and money, and write it into the budget.

**Voice and Representation.** Stand up one small advisory group with clear terms of reference and one real decision on its agenda. Month one: write one page of terms: purpose, how long people serve, time expected, how they are recognised. Month two: invite five or six people, including at least two you have not heard from before. Month three: hold the first meeting on a decision that is still open.

**Capability and Culture.** Build a simple induction for community members and a one-session briefing for staff. Month one: ask two community members what they wish they had been told at the start. Month two: write a two-page induction from their answers. Month three: run one briefing of an hour for staff and volunteers, and use the induction with the next person who joins.

**Engagement Practice.** Add one question to project planning: what decision is this, and what involvement does it warrant? Month one: add the question to whatever you use to plan a project, even an agenda. Month two: ask it of the next project before any activity is chosen. Month three: look back at what changed, and keep the question.

**Safety, Ethics and Recognition.** Adopt and publish a recognition and payment policy for community members, even a modest one. Month one: list what you ask of people now and what they receive. Month two: draft one page covering payment or recognition, costs, consent and who to contact if taking part is distressing. Month three: have the board adopt it, and put it on your website.

**Feedback and Learning.** Close one loop this month: go back to the last group consulted and tell them what happened to their input. Month one: send that message, including what you did not act on and why. Month two: start one shared document of what you have heard and what you did. Month three: make "tell them what happened" the last step of every activity.

**Outcomes and Impact.** Ask the community one question: what outcome would tell you we are doing our job? Start measuring it. Month one: ask it at your next event, group meeting or newsletter. Month two: choose one outcome from the answers that you can measure with what you already collect. Month three: report the first result to your board and your community.

4. Retake the review in 12 months.
