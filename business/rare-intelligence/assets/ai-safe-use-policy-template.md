**What this is:** The deliverable template for the AI Safe-Use Policy Sprint: a complete, adaptable AI use policy for a small Australian rare disease organisation, a one-page staff rule sheet, and a risk register with eight pre-filled example risks.
**Where it goes:** Adapted with each client during the two-week sprint, then adopted by the client's board. An outline version sits in the Signal Circle template library. Replace every [slot]; delete the guidance notes in italics before adoption.

> **Template, not legal advice.** This document is a starting point written for small rare disease organisations in Australia. Laws and AI tools change. Have the adapted policy reviewed by a qualified adviser before your board adopts it, and check the current guidance from the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) and the ACNC.

# PART A: AI USE POLICY

**[Organisation name]**
**Version:** [1.0] **Adopted by the board on:** [date] **Policy owner:** [role, for example Chief Executive] **Next review:** [date, no more than 12 months after adoption]

## 1. Purpose

[Organisation name] exists to [one-line purpose]. We hold the trust of people living with [condition or condition group] and their families. This policy sets out how we use artificial intelligence (AI) tools so that we save time on our work without putting that trust, anyone's privacy or the accuracy of our information at risk.

It has three aims:

1. Protect the personal and health information of the people we serve.
2. Make sure everything we publish or submit is accurate and is owned by a person.
3. Give staff and volunteers clear permission to use AI well, so that use is open and not hidden.

## 2. Scope

This policy applies to:

- Everyone who does work for [organisation name]: board members, staff, contractors, volunteers and students.
- All AI tools that generate or analyse text, images, audio or data, including general AI assistants (such as Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini or Copilot), AI features built into other software (email, documents, design, meeting and transcription tools), and any tool added in future.
- All work done for the organisation, on any device, including personal devices and personal accounts.

It does not cover clinical decision-making. [Organisation name] does not provide clinical care and does not use AI to give medical advice.

*Guidance: if your organisation does provide a health service, such as counselling or care coordination, say so here and take specific advice. Additional obligations are likely to apply.*

## 3. Principles

1. **People first.** We do not use AI in ways our community would be surprised or hurt to learn about.
2. **A person is always responsible.** AI produces drafts. A named person checks and owns every output.
3. **Privacy by default.** If in doubt, the information stays out.
4. **Honest about use.** We say when AI has been used, in line with section 8.
5. **Small steps.** We add tools and uses deliberately, one at a time, and review them.

## 4. Approved tools

Only tools on the approved list may be used for the organisation's work.

| Tool | Account type | Approved for | Settings required | Approved by and date |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Tool name] | [Organisation account] | [Green and amber information] | [For example: use of our content for model training switched off; chat history settings checked] | [Name, date] |
| [Tool name] | [Organisation account] | [Green information only] | [Settings] | [Name, date] |

Before a tool is approved, the policy owner checks and records: who provides it; where information is stored and processed; whether our information is used to train the provider's models and whether that can be switched off; who in the provider can see our content; how long content is kept and how it is deleted; and the cost.

*Guidance: record what the provider's current terms say on the date you check. Do not rely on memory or on what a tool did last year.*

Personal accounts are not to be used for amber information. Free versions of tools are treated as green only unless the policy owner has confirmed their settings in writing.

## 5. Approved uses

With an approved tool, and within the data rules in section 7, AI may be used to:

- Draft, edit, shorten or restructure text written by us: emails, newsletters, web pages, reports, social posts.
- Summarise published documents: research papers, government reports, consultation papers.
- Produce plain-language versions of published research for a person with relevant expertise to check.
- Draft sections of grant applications and funder reports from our own past material.
- Turn internal meeting notes into actions, where the notes contain no red information.
- Group de-identified survey responses or member input into themes.
- Draft first versions of submissions from de-identified input, subject to the evidence check in section 9.
- Plan projects, draft agendas, position descriptions and procedures.
- Check spelling, tone and readability, and translate drafts for a fluent speaker to review.

New uses not on this list are raised with the policy owner first.

## 6. Prohibited uses

AI must not be used to:

1. Process identifiable information about patients, families, members, donors, staff or volunteers (see section 7, red information).
2. Give, or appear to give, medical, legal or financial advice to any person.
3. Answer enquiries from families or the public without a person reading and sending the reply. No unsupervised chatbots.
4. Make or recommend decisions about individuals: eligibility for support, grants, employment, volunteering or membership.
5. Create quotes, stories, testimonials, case studies, statistics or references that are not real. Composite or illustrative stories must be labelled as such.
6. Create or alter images, audio or video of real people without their written consent, or create realistic images of patients who do not exist.
7. Record or transcribe a meeting or conversation without telling everyone present and having their agreement.
8. Produce content that promotes, names or compares a prescription medicine or other therapeutic product.
9. Analyse confidential information received from another organisation, including industry partners, funders or government, unless that organisation has agreed in writing.
10. Write board minutes or records of confidential discussions, unless the board has approved the tool and the process.

## 7. Data rules

Everyone classifies information before using AI. If you are unsure of the tier, treat it as red and ask.

| Tier | What it is | Examples | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| **GREEN: go** | Information that is already public, or that we would be comfortable publishing | Our website and published newsletters; published research papers and government reports; public consultation documents; general questions with no organisational detail | May be used in any approved tool |
| **AMBER: slow down** | Internal information that is not public but does not identify individuals from our community | Draft strategies and plans; unpublished grant applications with personal details removed; internal procedures; budgets without individual salaries; properly de-identified and grouped member input; meeting notes about operations | Only in tools approved for amber, on organisation accounts, with the required settings on. Remove names where they are not needed |
| **RED: stop** | Information that identifies a person, or could reasonably be used to identify one, and anything given to us in confidence | Names, contact details, dates of birth, photos, voice or video of patients, families, members or donors; health information, genetic information, diagnoses and care details; individual stories, even with the name removed, where the details could point to one family; membership and donor lists; staff and volunteer records; complaints; documents under a confidentiality agreement; committee papers given to anyone in confidence | Never entered into a general AI tool, in any account, for any purpose |

**De-identification in rare disease.** Removing a name is not enough. In a small community, a condition, an age, a state and a hospital can identify one child. Before information is treated as amber, remove or generalise names, exact ages, dates, places, hospitals, clinicians, family structure and unusual events, and ask: could a member of our community work out who this is? If yes, it is red.

**Legal context.** Health information and genetic information are sensitive information under the Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) and attract higher protection under the Australian Privacy Principles. Entering personal information into an AI tool may be a use or disclosure of that information, including a disclosure overseas. State and territory health records laws may also apply. *Guidance: do not assume the small business exemption applies to you; organisations that hold health information are often covered regardless of turnover. Confirm your position with an adviser and record the answer here: [slot].*

## 8. Human review and disclosure

**Human review.** Every AI-assisted output has a named person who:

- Reads the whole output, not a sample.
- Checks every fact, figure, name, date, quote and reference against its source.
- Removes anything that could be read as medical advice.
- Confirms the tone suits the audience, especially for newly diagnosed and bereaved families.
- Is recorded as the owner of the document.

Content about research, treatment or clinical care is also reviewed by a person with relevant expertise: [role or advisory group].

**Disclosure.** We are open about our use of AI.

- Our website carries this statement: "[Organisation name] uses AI tools to help draft and summarise some of our materials. A person checks everything we publish. We do not put identifiable information about our community into general AI tools."
- Substantially AI-drafted public materials carry a short note, for example: "Drafted with AI assistance and reviewed by [role]."
- Where a funder, government body or publisher has its own rule about AI use in applications or submissions, we follow it and declare use as required.
- If anyone asks whether AI was used in something we produced, we answer honestly.

## 9. Submissions and evidence

For any submission to government, a regulator, a health technology assessment process or a funder:

1. Member input is collected with consent that covers use in a submission, and is de-identified before any AI use.
2. Every factual claim in the draft is traced to either our own member input or a source a person has read. Claims that cannot be traced are removed.
3. Quotes are checked word for word against the original response.
4. The submission is approved by [role] before lodgement, and the check is recorded.

## 10. Working with industry partners

We work with pharmaceutical, biotechnology and other companies under [our partnership policy / the principles below]. In relation to AI:

1. Confidential information from a partner is red information. It is not entered into AI tools without the partner's written agreement.
2. We do not use AI to produce content about a partner's products, and we do not name, promote or compare therapeutic products in AI-assisted content.
3. A partner does not direct which AI tools we use, how we use them or what our AI-assisted materials say. Our submissions and communications remain our own.
4. Partners never receive identifiable information about our community, whether or not AI is involved. Any reporting to partners is aggregate.
5. Where a partner funds work in which AI is used, the funding is disclosed in plain words on the work.
6. We tell partners about this policy on request, and we ask partners who handle our information how they use AI with it.

*Guidance: member companies of Medicines Australia work under its Code of Conduct (Edition 20, in effect from 30 March 2025), which includes a section on support for patient organisations and public reporting of that support. Read the current Code and your partnership agreements when adapting this section.*

## 11. Incidents

An incident is any time red information is entered into an AI tool, an AI error reaches the public, or this policy is otherwise breached. Mistakes will happen. Reporting one quickly is always the right thing to do and will be treated that way.

1. **Stop.** Do not enter anything further. Do not try to fix it quietly.
2. **Tell** [named person and contact] as soon as possible and within 24 hours.
3. **Contain.** Delete the conversation or content from the tool where the tool allows it. Record what was entered, when, into which tool and which account.
4. **Assess.** The policy owner assesses what information was involved, who could be affected and how serious the harm could be. Under the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme, an organisation covered by the Privacy Act must assess a suspected eligible data breach promptly, and notify affected individuals and the OAIC where serious harm is likely. Take advice early. [Adviser contact.]
5. **Inform.** The policy owner tells the board chair. Where an error has reached the public, correct it openly and promptly. Consider whether any funder, partner or regulator must be told under an agreement or law.
6. **Learn.** Record the incident in the register, and change the rule sheet, training or approved tools as needed.

## 12. Responsibilities

| Who | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Board | Adopts this policy, reviews the risk register at least once a year, and receives incident reports. Board members have a duty to act with reasonable care and diligence under the ACNC Governance Standards; oversight of AI use is part of that |
| Policy owner: [role] | Keeps the approved tools list, approves new uses, runs induction and training, manages incidents |
| Everyone | Follows the rule sheet, classifies information before using AI, checks outputs, reports incidents |

## 13. Training

Everyone covered by this policy reads the one-page rule sheet and signs it before using AI for the organisation's work. The policy owner runs a short session at induction and once a year.

## 14. Review

This policy is reviewed every 12 months, and sooner if: the law changes; a tool's terms change in a way that affects our information; we add a significant new use; or there is a serious incident.

# PART B: ONE-PAGE STAFF RULE SHEET

*Print on one page. Pin it up. Everyone signs a copy.*

## Using AI at [organisation name]: the rules on one page

**Yes, you can use AI.** We would rather you used it openly and well than quietly.

**The seven rules**

1. **Never put red information into an AI tool.** Red means anything that identifies, or could identify, a patient, family member, member, donor, staff member or volunteer: names, contact details, dates of birth, photos, health details, individual stories. It also means anything given to us in confidence.
2. **Removing the name is not enough.** In our community, a condition, an age and a town can identify a child. If someone could work out who it is, it is red.
3. **Use approved tools on organisation accounts.** The list is at [location]. No personal accounts for internal work.
4. **You own what you send.** Read every word. Check every fact, figure, quote and reference against the source. AI can state invented things with confidence.
5. **No medical advice.** Nothing AI-assisted tells anyone what to do about their health. Anything about research or treatment is checked by [role].
6. **No invented people, quotes or numbers.** Ever.
7. **If something goes wrong, stop and tell [name] within 24 hours.** You will be thanked for reporting it.

**The traffic light**

| | What | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| **GREEN** | Public information, published research, our own published material | Any approved tool |
| **AMBER** | Internal, non-personal: drafts, plans, de-identified and grouped input | Approved tools for amber only, organisation account |
| **RED** | Anything about an identifiable person; anything confidential | Never |

**Not sure?** Treat it as red and ask [name, contact].

**Before you press send, ask three questions.** Is there anything red in here? Have I checked the facts? Would I be comfortable telling a member how this was made?
