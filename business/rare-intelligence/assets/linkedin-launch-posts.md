## Post 1: Tuesday 2 February

Two numbers I keep coming back to.

67% of Australian not-for-profits use generative AI. 14% have a policy or guideline for it. That is from Infoxchange's 2025 sector survey, as cited by PwC Australia in May 2026.

So in most organisations, people are using AI quietly, with no agreed rules.

In a rare disease organisation that matters more than most. You hold health stories. Your community is small enough that a condition and a town can identify a child. Your board is responsible for all of it.

The answer is not a ban. People will keep using it, less openly. The answer is a short set of rules that people will follow, and one or two workflows done properly.

If you run a rare disease organisation and want to know where you stand, I have built a ten-question quiz. About four minutes, instant result, no email needed. Link in the comments.

## Post 2: Thursday 4 February

If your organisation adopts one AI rule this month, make it this one.

Do not put identifiable patient or family information into a general AI tool.

Not names. Not dates of birth. Not photos. Not the email from a parent describing their child's week.

And in rare disease, removing the name is not enough. A condition, an age and a state can point to one family. Before anything goes into an AI assistant, ask: could someone in our community work out who this is? If yes, it stays out.

That single rule removes most of the risk. It also frees people to use AI openly for everything else: the grant section, the meeting notes, the newsletter.

I have written a free guide with five workflows that follow this rule, each with a prompt to copy and a checklist for the output. Link in the comments.

None of this is medical or legal advice. It is working practice from someone who has run one of these organisations.

## Post 3: Tuesday 9 February

A build story. The task: a new paper lands, and families want to know what it means.

The tool: an AI assistant such as Claude or ChatGPT.

The workflow: paste the paper's text. Ask for a 300-word summary under five headings, ending with "what this does not tell us yet" and "questions for your own doctor". Tell it to use only the text provided and to list every number with the sentence it came from.

The time saved: I will not give you a figure. Time it yourself the first time, including the checking.

The catch: AI makes findings sound more certain than they are. Twenty people becomes "patients". Mice become "a promising treatment". Families read these summaries with a great deal of hope, so the limitations section matters as much as the findings. A person with the right expertise checks it before it goes anywhere.

## Post 4: Thursday 11 February

AI's most dangerous habit is confidence.

It will give you a statistic, a quote or a reference that does not exist, in the same calm tone as one that does. In a consumer submission or a research summary for families, one invented fact can undo years of credibility.

So the skill worth learning is not prompting. It is checking.

A routine that works:
1. Mark every number, name, quote and reference in the draft.
2. Trace each one to a source you have read yourself.
3. Delete what you cannot trace.
4. Be most suspicious of the sentence that sounds best.

Written criteria help more than instinct. Decide what "good" means before you read the draft, not after.

## Post 5: Tuesday 16 February

A build story with higher stakes. The task: a consultation closes in ten days and you have 60 survey responses from members.

The workflow:
1. De-identify the responses yourself. Names, ages, places, hospitals, dates.
2. Give an AI assistant the consultation questions and the de-identified input.
3. Ask it to group what members said into themes, use their own words in short quotes, and add nothing of its own. Where published evidence would help, it writes "evidence needed" and you find it.
4. Run the evidence check. Every claim traces to a member response or a source you have read.

The time saved: time it yourself the first time.

The catch: the check is the workflow. The AI draft is only the starting material. If you do not have time to check for invented evidence and altered quotes, do not use AI for the submission.

A decision I made early, and why.

## Post 7: Tuesday 23 February

Five questions a board can ask about AI at its next meeting. None needs technical knowledge.

1. Who in our organisation is using AI, and for what?
2. What information are people never allowed to put into an AI tool, and is that written down?
3. Who checks AI-assisted work before it goes to members, funders or government?
4. If a member asked how we use AI, what would we say?
5. If someone made a mistake with personal information, who would they tell, and what would happen next?

If the honest answer to most of these is "we are not sure", you are in the majority. You are also one board paper away from fixing it.

A good AI policy for a small organisation is short: purpose, approved uses, prohibited uses, a traffic-light table for information, human review, disclosure, incident steps. Then one page for staff.
