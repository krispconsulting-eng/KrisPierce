# 5 AI workflows any rare disease charity can build this week

*Rare Intelligence. AI for rare-disease leaders.*

## Before you start

If you run a rare disease organisation, you are doing the work of a much larger team. You are also, probably, already using AI: a quick draft here, a summary there, with no agreed rules.

In Infoxchange's 2025 survey of not-for-profits in Australia and New Zealand, as cited by PwC Australia in May 2026, 67% were using generative AI and 14% had a policy or guideline for it.

This guide gives you five workflows you can build this week with a general AI assistant such as Claude or ChatGPT. Each has a prompt to copy, a checklist and the catch. There is always a catch.

Two honest notes. I will not tell you how many hours you will save: time it yourself the first time. And AI gives you a first draft; a person in your organisation still owns every word that goes out.

This guide is educational. It is not medical, legal or privacy advice.

---

## The safety rules (read this box first)

> **1. No identifiable patient or family information in general AI tools.** No names, dates of birth, photos, contact details, medical records or stories detailed enough to recognise someone. In rare disease the numbers are small: a condition, an age and a town can identify a child.
>
> **2. De-identify before you paste.** Remove names, places, dates, ages, hospital names and unusual details. If you could still work out who it is, so could someone else. Leave it out.
>
> **3. Check your tool's settings.** Find out whether the tool uses your conversations to train its models, and switch that off if you can. Use an account your organisation controls, not a personal one.
>
> **4. A person checks everything.** AI assistants can state invented facts, figures and references with complete confidence. Nothing goes to members, funders or government until someone who knows the subject has read it against the source.
>
> **5. AI does not give medical advice, and neither do you.** Summaries explain; they do not recommend. Point families back to their own clinicians.
>
> **6. Say when you used it.** If AI helped produce something public, be ready to say so plainly.

## Workflow 1: A research paper into a plain-language summary for families

**When to use it.** A new paper or trial result lands in your condition area and members start asking what it means.

**What you need.** The paper itself (full text if you have the right to use it, otherwise the abstract), and someone who can check the summary: a researcher, a clinician on your advisory group, or the paper's author.

**The steps.**

1. Read the abstract yourself first.
2. Paste the text into your AI assistant with the prompt below.
3. Read the draft against the paper, line by line.
4. Ask your checker one question: "Is anything here wrong or overstated?"
5. Publish with the paper's full reference and the date.

**Copy-and-paste prompt.**

```
You are helping a small patient organisation explain a research paper to families affected by [condition].

Using only the text I paste below, write a plain-language summary of about 300 words with these headings:
What the researchers wanted to find out
What they did (including how many people or animals were studied)
What they found
What this does not tell us yet
Questions you might ask your own doctor

Rules:
- Write for a tired parent reading on a phone. Short sentences. Explain any technical term the first time.
- Do not add any fact that is not in the text. If the paper does not say something, write "the paper does not say".
- Do not describe anything as a cure or a breakthrough. Do not give medical advice or suggest anyone change treatment.
- At the end, list every number you used and the sentence in the paper it came from, so I can check it.

Paper text:
[paste]
```

**What to check before you use it.**

- Every number matches the paper.
- The study size and type are stated (20 people is not 2,000; mice are not children).
- "What this does not tell us yet" is honest and has not been softened.
- No sentence could be read as treatment advice.
- Your checker has signed off.

**The catch.** AI tends to make results sound more certain and more relevant than they are. Families read these summaries with a great deal of hope, so the limitations section matters as much as the findings.

## Workflow 2: A first draft of a grant section from your own past applications

**When to use it.** You are writing the "about the organisation", "need" or "track record" section for the third time this year.

**What you need.** Two or three of your own past applications, the funder's question and word limit, and your current facts and figures.

**The steps.**

1. Remove anything confidential or personal from the past applications: named individuals, salaries, identifiable stories.
2. Paste the funder's question, the word limit and your past text with the prompt below.
3. Replace every placeholder the AI leaves with a current, real figure.
4. Rewrite the opening paragraph yourself, in your own voice.
5. Have a second person check it against the funder's guidelines.

**Copy-and-paste prompt.**

```
You are helping a small rare disease charity draft one section of a grant application.

The funder's question: [paste]
Word limit: [number]
What this funder cares about, from their guidelines: [paste or summarise]

Below are sections from our own past applications. Using only this material, draft a response to the question.

Rules:
- Use only facts and figures that appear in my material. Where a figure is needed and I have not given one, write [FIGURE NEEDED: describe what] instead of estimating.
- Answer the question asked, in the order it is asked.
- Plain, concrete language. No inflated claims.
- Stay within the word limit.
- After the draft, list any claims I should double-check because they may be out of date.

Our past material:
[paste]
```

**What to check before you use it.**

- Every figure is current and you can point to its source.
- The draft answers this funder's question, not last year's.
- No [FIGURE NEEDED] placeholders remain.
- Names of programs, partners and people are right.
- Nothing claims an outcome you cannot evidence.

**The catch.** Old figures travel. The AI will reuse a membership number from 2023 as readily as one from last month, and it cannot tell which is which. Treat every number as unverified until you have checked it.

## Workflow 3: Meeting notes into actions

**When to use it.** After any internal meeting where the notes are rough and nobody is sure who agreed to what.

**What you need.** Your typed notes or a transcript, with everyone's agreement if the meeting was recorded.

**The steps.**

1. Remove anything about identifiable patients, families or staff matters. If the meeting was mostly about a person, do not use AI for it.
2. Paste the notes with the prompt below.
3. Check the action list against your own memory of the meeting.
4. Send it to attendees within a day and ask for corrections.

**Copy-and-paste prompt.**

```
Below are rough notes from an internal meeting at a small charity. Turn them into:

1. Decisions made (one line each).
2. Actions, as a table: action, owner, due date. If the notes do not name an owner or a date, write "not agreed" rather than guessing.
3. Open questions that were raised and not resolved.
4. A three-sentence summary for someone who was not there.

Rules:
- Use only what is in the notes. Do not invent decisions, owners or dates.
- Keep people's wording where the meaning matters.

Notes:
[paste]
```

**What to check before you use it.**

- Each action has the right owner. This is where errors appear most often.
- "Not agreed" items are followed up, not quietly assigned.
- Nothing sensitive has slipped into the summary.

**The catch.** A tidy action list looks more authoritative than the messy conversation it came from. If the meeting did not decide something, the list should say so. Do not record meetings without telling people, and keep confidential board discussions out unless your board has agreed how.

## Workflow 4: A consumer submission first draft from de-identified member input

**When to use it.** A consultation is open, you have gathered members' experiences through a survey or conversations, and you have days, not weeks, to write the response.

**What you need.** Member input that has been de-identified, with consent for it to be used in a submission; the consultation questions; and a person who knows the evidence to do the final check.

**The steps.**

1. De-identify the input. Remove names, ages, places, dates, hospitals and any detail that could point to one family. In a very small community, consider combining responses into themes yourself before any AI is involved.
2. Paste the consultation questions and the de-identified input with the prompt below.
3. Run the evidence check: go through the draft and mark every factual claim. Each one must trace back to either your member input or a source you have read yourself.
4. Delete or correct anything that fails the check.
5. Share the draft with the members whose experiences shaped it, where you can.

**Copy-and-paste prompt.**

```
You are helping a patient organisation draft a response to a public consultation.

The consultation questions: [paste]

Below is de-identified input from our members. Using only this input, draft a response.

Rules:
- Group what members said into themes under each consultation question.
- Say how common each theme was in plain words ("most", "several", "one person"), based only on the input.
- Use members' own words in short quotes where they are strong. Do not alter quotes. Do not create quotes.
- Do not add statistics, studies, references or clinical claims of your own. If a point would be stronger with published evidence, write [EVIDENCE NEEDED: describe] and I will find it.
- Do not name or compare any medicine or product.
- At the end, list every statement in the draft that did not come directly from the member input.

Member input:
[paste]
```

**What to check before you use it.**

- Every quote is word for word from a real response.
- No statistic or reference appears that you did not supply and verify.
- Nobody could be identified from the combination of details.
- The draft reflects the range of what members said, including views that complicate the argument.
- A named person in your organisation has approved it.

**The catch.** This is the workflow with the most at stake. An invented study or a polished quote nobody said can discredit a submission and your organisation with it. The human check for invented evidence is the workflow. If you do not have time for it, do not use AI for the submission.

## Workflow 5: A month of social posts from one newsletter

**When to use it.** You have sent a good newsletter and your social channels have been quiet for three weeks.

**What you need.** The newsletter text (already public, so low risk), and a short description of your tone.

**The steps.**

1. Paste the newsletter with the prompt below.
2. Choose the posts that sound like you. Discard the rest without guilt.
3. Edit each one in your own voice and add the link or image.
4. Check that any story about a person has consent for social media, not only for the newsletter.
5. Schedule them across the month.

**Copy-and-paste prompt.**

```
Below is our latest newsletter. We are a small patient organisation for [condition group]. Our tone is warm, direct and never sensational.

Write 12 short social media posts drawn only from this newsletter, spread across these types: 4 that share one useful fact or resource, 3 that invite people to an event or action, 3 that thank or recognise someone, 2 that ask the community a question.

Rules:
- Each post under 80 words, with one clear point.
- Use only information in the newsletter. Do not add facts, dates or claims.
- No medical advice. No promises about research or treatment.
- No more than two hashtags per post.
- Mark each post with the newsletter section it came from.

Newsletter:
[paste]
```

**What to check before you use it.**

- Dates, times and links are right.
- Consent covers the channel you are posting on.
- Nothing has been simplified into a claim the newsletter did not make.
- The posts sound like your organisation, not like every other account.

**The catch.** AI-written posts drift towards the same cheerful sameness. Your community follows you for your voice. Use the drafts for structure and write the feeling yourself.
